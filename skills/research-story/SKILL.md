# research-story

[中文主稿](SKILL.zh.md). The Chinese guide is the authored source and this
entry is its synchronized English translation. Both carry the same workflow,
boundaries, requirements and budgets; the discovery filename grants no language
semantic priority. Sources and changes: [PROVENANCE.md](PROVENANCE.md).

## When to use

Use when results or a draft exist and the task is to find the strongest supported
claim, sequence the evidence or repair the paper's argument. Use
`research-question` when what should be tested or why it matters is unresolved,
an available manuscript-writing capability for a requested full prose draft, `prose-audit` for language
repair and `manuscript-review` for a complete manuscript audit. Story advice
cannot create missing findings, strengthen causal evidence or silently change
the accepted research direction.

## Method

1. Inspect the supplied material to the depth the claim requires. For an
   argument about a specific paper, read its relevant methods, results, figures,
   discussion and limitations; metadata or an abstract cannot establish the
   evidence ladder. Label the actual inspected coverage. If critical material is
   missing, work on a provisional narrative with named proof needs, not a final
   story diagnosis. A conceptual outline request may legitimately lack results.
2. Make an evidence inventory: observed outcomes, comparisons, negative results,
   assumptions, scope and unresolved alternatives. Distinguish the authors'
   interpretations from measurements or arguments. List the job of each existing
   figure/section: motivate a failure, define a construct, establish a pattern,
   distinguish mechanisms, test transfer or bound a claim. Extra figures need
   an argumentative role, not equal prominence by default.
3. Identify candidate central claims from a meaningful tension: what was
   uncertain, constrained or disputed, and what supplied evidence changes the
   judgment? Compare alternatives only when the material supports them. Prefer
   the claim whose consequence and support are clearest; a surprising phrasing
   is secondary to evidence. A narrow demonstrated claim can be stronger than a
   broad dramatic one that needs unseen data.
4. Build a story spine that moves the reader from question to warranted answer.
   Map each transition to `claim → evidence/figure → condition → unresolved rival`.
   Order evidence by what the next conclusion needs, not by laboratory chronology.
   Explain how the result changes what a field reader can understand, measure,
   choose or do; let editor/reviewer concerns inform stakes and proof, without
   treating all outputs as a journal pitch. Keep negative and limiting evidence
   that changes the conclusion.
5. Stress-test the chosen spine. Does the ending answer the original question?
   Is every causal or general claim supported at that strength? Would the
   strongest alternative explanation survive the reported comparison? Can the
   core point be expressed plainly without hiding necessary conditions? Separate
   “better expression” from “stronger contribution”; if evidence does not resolve
   the tension, return a provisional story and a discriminating proof need.
6. Apply only requested revisions: title/abstract direction, figure/section
   order, outline or specified prose. Preserve results, qualifiers and accepted
   scope. Record substantive changes and the reason; do not run a full language
   review unless needed. Check the revised sequence against the same evidence
   inventory, especially removed figures or counterevidence.

Maintain one requirements ledger for the accepted task and R1–R6. Record
`done`, `blocked` or `n/a` with checked evidence or a scope reason. Plans and
recommendations do not establish that downstream actions occurred.

## Requirements

- [B] R1 Actual source depth is stated — inspect relevant evidence and keep
  missing methods/results/figures separate from checked material.
- [B] R2 The central claim is bounded by evidence — trace its decisive supports,
  assumptions and relevant counterevidence.
- [B] R3 Figure/section order advances the argument — follow each transition's
  claim, supporting artifact, condition and unresolved rival.
- [B] R4 Narrative does not erase limits or create findings — compare the spine
  and revisions with the original inventory, numbers and qualifiers.
- [B] R5 The requested diagnosis/revision is delivered — check scope, language,
  target artifact and a useful decision or proof need.
- [A] R6 An alternate supported spine clarifies a real tradeoff — compare only
  if evidence or the request makes the alternative useful.

## When to stop

Use one inventory/story pass, one stress-test and at most one focused revision
with a recheck of changed claims. Deliver when the requested artifact and
blocking requirements pass. At the cap, report the supported spine, unfinished
revision or proof need and minimum material needed. An unresolved evidentiary
claim stays blocked even when its candidate wording is complete.

## Output contract

Deliver the requested diagnosis, spine or structural revision, a compact
claim-to-evidence/figure map, consequential change record and requirements
ledger. State source-depth limits and missing proof without inventing results.
Keep internal candidate history outside audience-facing manuscript prose.
